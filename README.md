# proDAD – A History of Innovation

Edition: 2026

## The Story of a Company Born from Curiosity

When a professionally produced video plays on a screen today, much of what we see seems almost effortless: Camera movements appear smooth and precise, text moves elegantly across the frame, transitions flow seamlessly, colors look right, distracting distortions disappear, and even difficult video footage can be corrected after the fact. Yet behind these seemingly effortless results lie mathematics, algorithms, image processing, and an enormous amount of technical detail. It was precisely at this intersection of visual design and highly complex software development that the story of proDAD began.

The story of this company did not begin with a business plan, an investor, or a market analysis. It began with a teenager who stopped in front of a blue screen in a department store. That teenager was **Holger Burkarth**. More than four decades later, the journey would span his first experiments on a Commodore 64, the Amiga and Casablanca video systems, and modern techniques for video stabilization, rolling-shutter correction, object tracking, optical flow, GPU processing, 360° video, and noise reduction. At the same time, the story of proDAD GmbH is also the story of two very different yet complementary personalities: **Holger Burkarth**, who wanted to understand technical problems down to their lowest level and turn them into software, and **Andreas Huber**, who took responsibility for the commercial, organizational, and financial side of the company. What united them was their willingness to try something even when nobody could guarantee that it would work.

### The Toolbox for Video Makers: The proDAD Product World Today

To understand how proDAD has shaped digital video editing, it is worth taking a look at its current product portfolio. Each application addresses specific, extremely demanding challenges in modern image processing and is distinguished by unique technological capabilities.

*Mercalli* is regarded as a worldwide benchmark for image stabilization. The software corrects shaky footage, drone jitters, and CMOS distortions such as the rolling-shutter effect using motion vectors. *Vitascene* provides cinematic color filters, lighting effects, and fluid transitions for visual enhancement, calculated in real time on the GPU. *Heroglyph* covers typographic text design and travel-route animation, providing control over paths, titles, trailers, and lower thirds. With *Respeedr*, extremely smooth slow motion and time-lapse sequences can be created because the program calculates entirely new intermediate frames through precise optical-flow analysis.

*Defishr* can fully automatically correct the distortion caused by fisheye lenses on action cameras, while *Spherixr* is designed for editing spherical 360° video. In the field of intelligent image analysis, *Disguise* and *Hide* set benchmarks: *Disguise* enables reliable tracking for anonymizing faces, while *Hide* seamlessly removes unwanted objects. Finally, *Denoisr* removes image noise from low-light footage without sacrificing fine details.

All solutions can be used both as standalone applications and as integrated plug-ins in all major professional editing systems.

### The Architect Behind the Scenes: A Look Behind the Curtain

Behind this impressive product portfolio lies an extraordinary success story. Founded by **Andreas Huber** and **Holger Burkarth**, proDAD was built from the very beginning on a clear and highly effective synergy: While **Andreas Huber** assumed commercial and organizational responsibility, **Holger Burkarth** devoted himself entirely to his lifelong passion—the deep understanding of how things work at their core and the transformation of even the most complex technical problems into functioning software.

What continues to define proDAD at its core is a fundamental characteristic: **Holger Burkarth** is the technological soul behind the products. He never operated merely as a specialized developer responsible for one part of a system. Instead, he consistently understood the entire system—from the underlying mathematical equations and data structures to processor-level machine-language programming (Assembly, C++, C#), GPU kernels, and the visual representation on the screen.

This biography tells the fascinating story of a pioneer who, more than four decades ago, stood fascinated in front of the blue screen of a Commodore C64 in a department store. It is the story of a self-taught developer who never allowed technical obstacles, a lack of capital, or inadequate hardware to stop him. When a tool was missing, he programmed it himself. When a processor was too slow, he invented new algorithms to make the impossible possible after all.

Join us on a journey through the decades of computer history: From the 8-bit era of the 1980s and the glorious Amiga years, through the risky step into full-time self-employment with the revolutionary SSA technology (*ClariSSA*), to today's high-performance algorithms for modern image processing.

It is a story of perseverance, iron determination in bringing visionary ideas to life, and an unwavering pursuit of innovation: The story of **Holger Burkarth** and proDAD.

## Chapter 1: The Spark in the Department Store – How a Blue Screen Began a Life Journey

On a cold, wet winter day in 1983, seventeen-year-old Holger was in the middle of his apprenticeship as a mechanic—a solid hands-on profession that demanded precision, an understanding of mechanics, and a feel for how moving parts worked together. Once a week he attended vocational school and, as so often, spent his lunch break with several classmates at the nearby Karstadt department store. It was the usual walk between household goods and consumer electronics, until Holger suddenly stopped in front of a small demonstration stand.

On a simple CRT television, a deep-blue screen flickered, displaying nothing more than a light-blue border and the cryptic lines

`**** COMMODORE 64 BASIC V2 ****`

`64K RAM SYSTEM 38911 BASIC BYTES FREE`

For most passersby, it was a lifeless and incomprehensible display, but to Holger it exerted a magical attraction. At that moment, another visitor stepped up to the stand and began typing on the brown breadbox keyboard. Within moments, the screen came alive: Lines moved across the monitor, colors flickered, and sounds rang out. For Holger, it was a revelation. For the first time, he saw a microcomputer in action—the legendary Commodore 64.

The concept of a computer was not entirely new to him, however. He had enthusiastically watched glowing consoles in science-fiction movies and had already been fascinated by autonomous systems for years. At the age of twelve, he had spent hours leafing through the Quelle catalog, not looking for toys but simply out of an interest in new technological devices, until his attention finally became captivated by a small, solar-powered pocket calculator. Its high price initially made the object seem unattainable. When his parents eventually gave it to him for Christmas, he used the manual to learn about trigonometric functions such as sine and cosine for the first time. While other children regarded math problems as an annoying chore, Holger discovered the fascination of mathematics as a language for describing the world.

At thirteen, his first major project followed: He wanted to equip his bicycle with an automatic gear-shifting system. The movement of the gear levers seemed like a problem he could solve, but the question was how the system itself should decide when to shift up or down. In the children's educational series *“Wie geht das?”* (“How Does It Work?”), he had read that computers consisted of logic gates such as AND and OR gates. So he promptly took an A3 drawing pad from school and began drawing his own computer, gate by gate, with pencil and ruler. He knew that he would never be able to solder the necessary semiconductors himself, but the conceptual foundation had been laid: He wanted to understand how pure logic could be transformed into automation.

Now that dream stood before him in real life at the department store. His apprenticeship wages as a trainee mechanic were modest, and much of the money was already spent on his monthly bus pass. Nevertheless, his decision was firm: He had to own a C64.

For four months before the purchase, Holger spent his free time in the local public library looking for programming literature. The selection was extremely limited; all he could find was a thin paperback with a dark-blue cover about the BASIC programming language. Holger devoured its roughly 50 pages and memorized the commands before he had even touched a single key on a real computer.

In the winter of 1984, the time had finally come. His savings were exactly enough for a Commodore 64, but the salesperson enthusiastically tried to sell him a VC1541 disk drive as well. Since the drive cost almost as much as the computer itself and was therefore unaffordable for an apprentice, he reluctantly opted for the cheaper alternative: a Datasette, which stored data on ordinary audio cassettes.

When he got home, there was no desk of his own, so the C64 had to be connected directly to the family's television in the living room. After returning from work at around 6 p.m., Holger sat on the floor with his eyes fixed on the screen, typing lines of code. Every evening, before the 8 p.m. news, the entire setup had to be dismantled and put away so that his parents could watch television undisturbed. Only after he had saved enough money to buy a small black-and-white television did his realm move into his own room, where a small table, a television, and the C64 were enough for him to work for hours.

The painful limitations of the Datasette became apparent very quickly, as saving and loading programs seemed to take forever. Even worse were the dreaded crashes: A minor error or a fluctuation in the power supply could irretrievably erase his work from volatile RAM. It was not until his third year of training that his slightly higher wages finally enabled him to buy the long-awaited VC1541 floppy drive, making loading times bearable and allowing data to be stored securely on 5.25-inch floppy disks.

Holger devoured specialist magazines such as the *“Commodore 64 Magazin”*. Although he initially programmed in BASIC, he soon ran into performance limitations. BASIC was an interpreted language, slow and sluggish, and completely unsuitable for complex graphics calculations. A magazine article put it plainly: To unleash the full power of the C64's MOS 6510 processor, programming had to be done in the chip's native language—in machine language, or Assembly.

Commercial assembler software was expensive and beyond his means, and this sparked Holger's ambition: If he could not afford an assembler, he would simply have to program one himself. Since he did not yet have an editor, he looked up the hexadecimal codes of processor instructions in manuals, manually translated mnemonics such as `LDA`, `STA`, and `JMP` into byte sequences, and calculated branch distances for relative instructions such as `BNE` and `BEQ` in order to write his first machine-code program. He initially programmed a simple assembler in BASIC. Although it was slow, it was sufficient to develop a second, far more powerful assembler written entirely in native machine language. This was a decisive milestone: Holger had created his own tool, one that worked at lightning speed and gave him absolute control over the hardware.

There were hardly any like-minded people around him; only two acquaintances, Alex and Andreas, also owned a C64. When they met, everything revolved around computer games and computer graphics, while Holger was passionate about the technology behind them. Together, the idea emerged to develop their own video game, with Alex and Andreas designing the graphics while Holger programmed the technical engine.

Using his self-written assembler, Holger developed, by the standards of the time, a smoothly scrolling playfield on which objects could be positioned precisely and checked for collisions. While Holger delved ever deeper into hardware programming of the VIC-II graphics chip, the group gradually lost interest and the project petered out. The knowledge he gained about graphics routines and performance optimization, however, would pay off later.

Holger's curiosity was not limited to software. He was deeply fascinated by an article about a light pen—an input device that allowed users to draw directly on the CRT screen, since a computer mouse for the C64 practically did not exist at the time. Following the published circuit diagram, he picked up a soldering iron, installed a photodiode in the tip of an empty ballpoint pen, and wired it to the C64's control port. When the electron beam of the CRT passed the photodiode, it triggered a signal that Holger captured in software to calculate the exact cursor position on the screen. It worked perfectly, and the pointer followed the movement of his hand precisely across the glass of the television.

Elated by this success, he turned to his next hardware project: a homemade plotter that would draw graphics on paper with pens. But this time fate struck mercilessly. While wiring his own circuit board, he made a disastrous wiring mistake, sending a short circuit through the C64's mainboard and destroying sensitive custom chips. The screen remained black, and the repair costs pushed the young apprentice to the brink of financial ruin, as they were almost equivalent to the price of a new machine. It was a painful setback, but it led to a clear decision: In the future, he would focus primarily on software development.

Instead of giving up, he devoted himself to the C64's internal system software, writing analysis and monitor programs as well as disassemblers to examine the computer's memory and analyze third-party programs. He optimized the C64's kernel routines and developed his own fast-loader algorithms, significantly increasing the serial transfer speed between the C64 and the VC1541 floppy drive through a modified handshake strategy.

He was equally fascinated by geometric calculations—for example, how to rotate points, lines, and simple 3D wireframe models and draw them moving in real time on the screen. On an 8-bit processor clocked at only 1 MHz and without an FPU (floating-point unit), this required extremely sophisticated fixed-point arithmetic and mathematical tricks. At the same time, he experimented with the SID sound chip (MOS 6581) and programmed his own small sound editors for synthesizing music and effects.

What had begun in 1983 with a fascinated glance at a blue screen had, within just a few years, developed into a deep and uncompromising passion. Holger had not merely learned how to program; he had also learned how to push hardware to its absolute limits, how to extract maximum performance from minimal resources, and how to translate complex problems into elegant code—laying the foundation for the later success of proDAD.

## Chapter 2: The Spark from Fokus-Film and the Rise of Adorage (1987–1991)

When Commodore ushered in a new era of personal computing with the Amiga in 1986, Holger's fascination with the C64 quickly gave way to a desire for new horizons. In 1987, he moved to the Amiga's advanced 16/32-bit architecture. The system's graphics and sound capabilities were breathtaking by the standards of the time. But Holger was not content merely to use the machine as an end user. Through meticulous self-education, he learned about the Amiga's custom chips—Denise, Agnus, and Paula—and programmed his own real-time 2D and 3D graphics routines. To understand the workings of digital image processing down to the last bit, he even wrote his own version of the famous DPaint graphics software.

The decisive turning point came in 1989, when the film production company *Fokus-Film* moved into the vicinity of his home. When Holger learned about a special assignment, he took on a freelance development project in his spare time: He was to create a spectacular digital video transition effect for their productions.

At that time, computer animations on home computers suffered from distracting stuttering and uneven frame rates. Holger worked intensively on a solution that would make motion on Amiga hardware appear extremely smooth and natural. Within just one week, he succeeded in implementing it. The result was a video transition with a level of visual quality and smoothness that had previously been considered impossible on home computers. Andreas was also thrilled by the achievement. This technical breakthrough led Holger and Andreas to a clear conclusion: This potential had to become a product of its own.

For the commercial foundation, Holger worked with his longtime friend Andreas Huber. They established a clear and highly effective division of responsibilities that would shape the company for decades. Andreas took over overall commercial management, finances, and organization. In doing so, he created a protected environment in which Holger Burkarth could devote one hundred percent of his attention to what he did best: developing groundbreaking software products.

The company was officially founded at the beginning of 1990 under the name proDAD. Even the iconic company logo bore Holger's unmistakable signature. At the time, he was experimenting with controlling an HP3 laser printer through its integrated drawing syntax. Rather than drawing the logo by hand, he decided to construct and program the lettering from commands. He first recreated the HP3 language on the Amiga so that he could use its graphics commands more flexibly. For the logo, he imposed a set of rules on himself: The outline of the symbol had to consist of a single, uninterrupted line that could not cross itself at any point. This logical-geometric approach produced the distinctive proDAD lettering, which was later complemented by the characteristic wings and shadows.

Based on the effect developed for Fokus-Film, Holger began working on a standalone product. Users were to be able to add impressive transition effects to video sequences and play them back smoothly. Alongside his main job, Holger spent every free minute programming the software.

At the end of 1990, the two founders traveled to the Amiga trade show in Cologne to demonstrate their prototype to industry experts. The response was overwhelming. Even on the exhibition floor, they entered into direct discussions with representatives of Commodore and established software distributors. It was these industry professionals who eventually suggested the right name for the debut product: *“Adorage.”*

Another year of hard development work followed until the software was ready for the market. At the 1991 Amiga trade show, *Adorage* made its official debut at the booth of its then-distributor. The distributor had produced boxes of product packaging in advance, but demand exceeded all expectations. In less than two days, the entire inventory had sold out. For the remainder of the show, only handwritten pre-orders could be accepted.

Crowds gathered at the demonstration booth, captivated as Holger Burkarth tirelessly demonstrated the capabilities of *Adorage* and explained technical details. Meanwhile, Andreas Huber established valuable contacts with business partners. This trade-show event provided the final proof of the product's market potential and laid the foundation for proDAD to separate from its distributor the following year and operate independently as a systems house.

## Chapter 3: 1992 – The Year of Risk and Technological Revolution

The overwhelming success of *Adorage* had opened a door that could no longer be closed. In 1992, the founders faced the pivotal decision of their lives: Should they continue on the secure path of their existing careers, or take the risk of running proDAD full-time from then on?

It was a step filled with risk. In the founders' rural home region, computers were still “foreign territory” to most people in the early 1990s, and the economic significance of software development was difficult to grasp. Their familiar surroundings and even their own parents strongly advised caution in view of the uncertainty. But in the end, the pioneering spirit and unwavering belief in their own abilities prevailed over the need for security. Guided by the pragmatic motto, “We'll give it a try for two years and then see whether it works,” they threw themselves into the adventure of self-employment.

The vision was clear: The innovative animation technology that Holger had already incorporated into *Adorage* in rudimentary form was to be developed into a standalone, high-performance system. The goal was extremely ambitious: A tool should be able to convert any animation into a new, extremely smooth format and play it back without stuttering. Thus, SSA technology (Super Smooth Animation) was born. Andreas named the planned flagship product *“ClariSSA”*—a name that combined its elegant sound with the technological superiority of the SSA engine.

But the path from idea to market launch became an exhausting struggle against technical limitations, time pressure, and physical exhaustion. During the intensive development work, Holger encountered a fundamental obstacle: The combination of **GFA-BASIC** and inline Assembly he had previously used proved unsuitable and too inflexible for the planned, highly complex system extensions.

At this critical stage, Holger made a radical and courageous decision: He abandoned the existing foundation and decided, in the middle of the ongoing development process, to switch to ANSI C. Under the relentless pressure of the upcoming Photokina trade show in September 1992, he forced himself to learn a completely new, highly structured programming language through self-study and immediately use it to build an architecturally sophisticated software product.

A period of profound social isolation and extreme focus followed. The boundaries between day and night disappeared. Holger spent up to 18 hours a day in front of the monitor, writing algorithms, optimizing memory structures, and integrating C code with highly optimized machine-language routines.

The enormous mental and physical strain was compounded by existential financial pressure. Producing high-quality product boxes and manuals consumed enormous sums of money. Then an unexpected tax bill, together with substantial advance tax payments demanded by the tax office, almost completely exhausted the company's start-up capital. Every mark had to be counted twice. When the gates of the Cologne exhibition halls opened for Photokina, proDAD's economic existence was at stake.

But on the very first day of the trade show, the paralyzing tension turned into pure triumph. The proDAD booth became a major attraction and was practically overrun by trade visitors. Visitors could hardly believe their eyes: The monitors displayed graphic animations with a level of smoothness and frame rate previously known only from extremely expensive Silicon Graphics workstations or professional film productions. A digitized sample sequence from the film *“Terminator 2”* caused a sensation and provided irrefutable proof that this level of quality had now become a reality on affordable home computers.

Just how far ahead of the industry proDAD was became apparent in a remarkable moment at the booth. A developer from the competing company MainConcept, which was also presenting an animation tool, asked whether his own test demo could be played through the SSA engine. When the manager saw the result on the screen, he was so astonished by the fluid presentation that he immediately called over his entire development team. Together, the competing specialists marveled at the technical achievement—they agreed that Holger Burkarth had created something that appeared to transcend the limits of the hardware of the time.

*ClariSSA* sold out completely at the trade show within a very short time. The team had to take countless pre-orders directly at the booth. Back home, the company founders became logistics workers: By hand, they painstakingly copied, labeled, and bundled hundreds of 3.5-inch floppy disks with the printed manuals, packed them into book-sized soft boxes, and took them to the post office to process the flood of orders.

*ClariSSA* was far more than an economic breakthrough. It cemented the reputation of proDAD and Holger Burkarth as a technological spearhead in smooth video and graphics processing and laid the foundation for a company that would repeatedly redefine the boundaries of what was visually possible in the decades that followed.

## Chapter 4: Monument Titler and the Casablanca Era – The Leap to Dedicated Hardware (1994–1998)

Following the impressive triumphs of *Adorage* and *ClariSSA*, the Amiga world faced dramatic upheaval in the mid-1990s. Its parent company, Commodore, fell into turmoil and eventually filed for bankruptcy. While many developers watched helplessly as the Amiga ecosystem approached collapse, Holger remained relentlessly focused on advancing visual tools. Video authors and Amiga editors were no longer satisfied with smooth transitions alone; they were demanding professional titles, stylish fonts, and dynamic picture-in-picture effects.

It was in this dynamic market environment that a new vision took shape in 1994: the *Monument Titler*, which would later be developed further as *Monument Designer*. Holger's declared goal was to create a powerful, standalone titling system capable of building text directly on the screen in previously unattainable visual quality and moving it through space.

The technical challenge of high-end title rendering was enormous, however, because it collided with the extremely limited main-memory resources of Amiga computers at the time. Systems typically had only a few megabytes of RAM available, in which graphics buffers, font resources, and program code all had to coexist. Memory-intensive techniques were therefore ruled out from the outset. Instead of computationally intensive vector fonts, Holger opted for bitmap fonts. However, these quickly developed unattractive, staircase-like pixel edges when scaled or rotated. To fundamentally eliminate this visual problem, he developed a collection of independent, highly efficient algorithms.

First, Holger designed a special anti-aliasing algorithm for smoothing edges along pixel contours that precisely analyzed the outlines and edges of bitmap letters. The system calculated the smoothing exactly at the transitions between pixel contours, allowing fonts to remain razor-sharp and free of distracting fraying even at extreme magnifications. To move typography and graphics freely through space, he also developed an extremely fast real-time shading and scaling algorithm. It could rotate bitmap graphics through arbitrary angles and scale them continuously without producing unattractive staircase effects or visual artifacts. Despite the hardware's severe memory limitations, the algorithm's highly optimized memory access made it possible to display the entire text rendering in real time on Amiga hardware. This was an invaluable advantage for video editors who wanted to evaluate the result of their designs immediately on the monitor. With the *Monument Titler*, Holger not only laid the technological foundation for the later successful product *Heroglyph*, but once again demonstrated his ability to overcome fundamental hardware limitations through algorithmic elegance.

The year 1994 also marked an important milestone for Holger in terms of software architecture. Driven by his insatiable thirst for knowledge and his desire to make software more structured and maintainable, he had already spent more than a year intensively studying object-oriented programming in C++ through technical literature and self-study. Until then, the Amiga platform simply lacked the right tool for using C++ productively in everyday development. This changed abruptly with the release of the *Maxon C++ Compiler*, the first truly mature and usable C++ compiler for the Amiga system. Holger did not hesitate and, beginning with the development of the *Monument Titler*, made the complete transition from ANSI C to C++. Object-oriented programming allowed him to organize complex systems such as graphics engines, font objects, and user interfaces into clean, reusable class structures. This solid C++ knowledge would become the steel backbone of all future proDAD development over the coming decades.

As the classic home-computer market for the Amiga gradually collapsed, the German company *MacroSystem* entered the scene. *MacroSystem* recognized the untapped potential of the Amiga architecture for dedicated video editing and developed a revolutionary hardware concept: the *Casablanca*, a standalone set-top video editing system for the living room and semi-professional video studio. The *Casablanca* deliberately dispensed with a traditional PC keyboard or a complex operating system. It connected directly to a television or studio monitor and was conveniently operated with a trackball or mouse. Inside, however, beat the heart of proven Amiga hardware, combined with specialized custom chips and hard drives for digital video. For proDAD, this new hardware platform represented a crucial strategic shift: away from the shrinking hobby-computer market and toward the rapidly growing market for dedicated video-editing hardware.

Adapting proDAD software to the *Casablanca*—particularly *Monument Designer* and the popular *Adorage* effects library—presented Holger with entirely new challenges. Since the *Casablanca* did not run a standard Workbench operating system, he had to adapt his C++ engines and graphics routines so that they could work directly with the specialized hardware and the proprietary *Casablanca* operating system, *Bogart*. Thanks to its extremely frugal use of main memory and the immense speed of his shading algorithm, *Monument Designer* reached its absolute peak on *Casablanca* systems between 1997 and 1998. Video editors around the world began using the software extensively to enhance vacation films, documentaries, and wedding videos with professional titles, transitions, and visual effects. Its lasting success on the *Casablanca* not only secured proDAD's economic survival during a turbulent period of upheaval in the computer industry, but once again demonstrated Holger's exceptional ability to turn visionary ideas into functioning, market-leading software through relentless determination.

## Chapter 5: The Vision of a New Foundation – The pOS Adventure (1995–1998)

By the mid-1990s, the inevitable end of an era was becoming apparent. The slow decline of Commodore and the threatened liquidation of the entire Amiga ecosystem filled many developers with deep concern. For Holger, however, this dramatic upheaval meant much more than simply losing a familiar working environment. For years, he had been fascinated by the elegant architecture of AmigaOS—an operating system that offered true multitasking, extremely low latency, and a remarkably lean structure despite requiring minimal hardware resources. The unbearable thought that these pioneering ideas and principles might disappear into the depths of computer history sparked an enormous drive to act.

Driven by this inner motivation, Holger embarked on an extraordinarily bold project in 1995: creating a completely new operating system under the name *pOS*, short for proDAD Operating System. It was a project of almost unimaginable scale, one that would normally have required the resources of entire development teams and major corporations. Yet from 1995 to 1998, Holger tirelessly set about programming this monumental undertaking layer by layer, almost entirely on his own.

Holger designed *pOS* as a modern, highly modular operating system. His declared goal was broad compatibility with existing AmigaOS concepts, allowing users and developers to make a seamless transition. At the same time, he wanted to overcome the historical shortcomings of the old platform and create a future-proof foundation capable of running hardware-independently on modern processors. During countless day-and-night sessions, he developed all of the system's core components on his own. At the heart of the software was a preemptive multitasking kernel with extremely fast message exchange for inter-process communication, highly optimized memory management, and sophisticated process scheduling. In parallel, he constructed his own abstraction layers for storage media and hardware drivers, making the file system flexible enough to adapt to a wide variety of processor architectures. He also developed the graphical user interface entirely from scratch: He effectively built it in C++ and embedded an object-oriented windowing system with full event handling, dynamic rendering, and intuitive user interaction. For the technical community, *pOS* was something of a sensation, because Holger impressively demonstrated that a single developer with iron discipline, deep systems knowledge, and an uncompromising determination to implement his ideas could create a functioning, high-performance operating system.

Holger's unwavering hope and the driving force behind this enormous undertaking lay in his deep belief in the community. He firmly believed that many talented developers and fellow enthusiasts from the Amiga scene would seize the opportunity to build a new, independent software ecosystem on modern hardware together. *pOS* was intended to become the technological home for everyone who wanted to carry the spirit of the Amiga era into the future.

But as the months and years passed, the initial optimism gave way to a painful realization. In numerous intensive discussions with other developers, studios, and industry insiders, Andreas repeatedly encountered the same sobering response. The original pioneering spirit of the early computer era had changed. Instead of enthusiasm for a shared, independent foundation, the pragmatic question of personal financial benefit dominated almost every conversation. The critical mass of developers and third-party manufacturers willing to invest time and resources in porting their applications to *pOS* simply failed to materialize. The realization was bitter but unavoidable: Without a broad software ecosystem and without a sustainable community, a one-person operating system had no chance of surviving in the market.

In 1998, proDAD reluctantly made the strategic decision to discontinue the *pOS* project. Although the dream of creating its own operating system remained unfulfilled, the three years of development work had by no means been wasted. Through his masterful handling of this Herculean task, Holger had acquired an invaluable body of knowledge about system architecture, memory management, and object-oriented C++ design—a foundation that would become the bedrock for the subsequent successes on the Windows PC.

## Chapter 6: Ahead of Its Time – The Vision of Video Telephony and the Lessons of Cocktel (1997)

The year 1997 marked an extraordinary period in which proDAD ventured into technological territory that still seemed like pure science fiction to the vast majority of computer users. While Holger was working on the *pOS* operating system and video-editing software was flourishing on the Casablanca systems, he became fascinated by a completely new and seemingly impossible challenge: transmitting live video and audio synchronously over an ordinary analog telephone line.

The basic idea was simply revolutionary. Years before broadband Internet, modern video-conferencing platforms, or smartphones had become part of everyday life, proDAD wanted to connect people visually across great distances. Under the fitting name *Cocktel*, Holger developed a highly complex complete system for live video telephony on the Amiga.

From a technical perspective, the project was a daring ride on a razor's edge. The conditions in 1997 were brutally restrictive. Instead of fast DSL or ISDN, households had only ordinary analog copper telephone lines and standard modems capable of maximum transfer rates of a meager 28.8 or 33.6 kbit/s. Both the compressed video image and the audio signal had to be transmitted in real time over this extremely narrow bandwidth, without the connection dropping or latency making conversation impossible.

Holger tackled the mathematical foundations of signal processing and data compression. Since ready-made software components for this extreme situation simply did not exist, he developed custom algorithms from scratch. To reduce the video image to just a few kilobytes per second, he wrote an intelligent difference-based method. Instead of transmitting complete individual frames, only those pixel regions that changed from one frame to the next were transmitted, combined with aggressive but visually tolerable scaling to low resolutions. At the same time, the voice signal had to be digitized, heavily compressed through efficient audio quantization, and transmitted in perfect synchronization over the same modem interface to ensure reliable lip sync. Using a connected video and audio digitizer, the software captured the analog camera signal, compressed it at lightning speed on the Amiga's main processor, and passed the data packets directly to the modem's serial interface. To technically knowledgeable observers, *Cocktel* was an algorithmic masterpiece, because Holger had demonstrated that synchronous live video telephony was technically feasible even on aging Amiga hardware and over simple telephone cables.

In the fall of 1997, proDAD proudly presented the system at the Cologne computer trade show, but a cold shower followed at the booth. While earlier video-effects software such as *Adorage* and *ClariSSA* had attracted streams of visitors, interest in video telephony was virtually nonexistent. The proDAD team faced a classic chicken-and-egg problem, which disappointed visitors repeatedly expressed in the same sobering question: Who exactly were they supposed to call if nobody else owned such a system? A communication tool only becomes practically useful once a critical mass of users has the same hardware and software. In 1997, however, there was neither widespread network infrastructure nor broad public awareness of digital video communication. For most Amiga users, the costly purchase of camera digitizers and modems solely for private conversations was simply uneconomical, making *Cocktel* the first and only commercial failure in proDAD's Amiga history.

The setback became particularly painful in retrospect when, around six years later in 2003, the small company Skype stormed into the world of video telephony on the Windows PC and shortly afterward changed hands for billions. By then, however, the conditions were completely different: PCs had processors that were many times more powerful, while widespread ISDN and the first DSL connections offered bandwidth that Holger could only have dreamed of in 1997. The mathematical and programming challenges Skype had to solve in the era of gigahertz processors were hardly comparable to the pioneering achievements Holger had accomplished years earlier on the Amiga. For Andreas and Holger, however, the failure of 1997 had a lasting consequence: Disappointed by the market's lack of readiness, they lost interest in the segment and returned their full attention to their core competencies—image-based video editing, titling, and specialized visual effects. In retrospect, *Cocktel* became the classic example of a bold vision that was simply years ahead of its time. The technical feasibility had been masterfully demonstrated, but the world simply was not yet ready for the future of video telephony.

## Chapter 7: The End of a Pioneer Era – Leaving the Amiga and Realigning the Company (1998)

The years between 1990 and 1998 formed an era of new beginnings, uninhibited experimentation, and pure fascination with digital pioneering. It was a time when computer culture was young, boundaries seemed infinitely distant, and a single highly focused developer with ingenious algorithms could astonish the established industry. In this gold-rush atmosphere, numerous commercial software solutions flowed from Holger's hands, enriching the Amiga ecosystem and extending their influence far beyond its boundaries.

Beginning with the early successes of *Adorage* and its successors *Adorage AGA* and *Adorage v2*, through the revolutionary SSA technology in *ClariSSA v1*, *ClariSSA v2*, and *ClariSSA v3*, Holger repeatedly demonstrated his exceptional capabilities in smooth animation rendering. This product world was complemented by specialized tools such as the color quantizer *Transformer* and the image-conversion tool *Konrad*. For professional video typography and graphic design, he created the powerful *Monument Titler v1* and *Monument Titler v2*, which ultimately evolved into *Monument Designer v1* and *Monument Designer v2* for the Amiga, Draco, and Casablanca platforms.

But Holger's drive to develop knew no system boundaries. With *Animage*, he developed a real-time compositing video editor, while tools such as *CAVIN* revolutionized automatic camcorder control in video editing. Even infrastructure and communication solutions such as the serial networking tool *pNet*, the video-telephony software *Cocktel*, and ultimately the ambitious *pOS* operating-system project came from his work during this era.

Until the very end, Andreas and Holger held on to the hope that the Amiga might have a future through rescue efforts or new owners. But the mismanagement of parent company Commodore and the absence of a clear hardware strategy ultimately took their relentless toll. By 1998, reality could no longer be denied: The once-proud Amiga ecosystem was history.

For proDAD, this marked a dramatic turning point. The foundation on which the company had grown and celebrated its first major successes no longer existed. To secure the company's economic survival and carry its accumulated technological expertise into the future, Andreas and Holger had to completely reinvent themselves. Their focus turned to a new architecture, a new operating system, and a market that operated according to its own rules: the Windows PC. A new chapter in the company's history was about to begin.
